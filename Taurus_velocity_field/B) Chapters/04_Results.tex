\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:Results}

\section{The Reconstructed Velocity Field}
Firstly, a short clarification on the following results: given the small sample
of stars that the catalog of the TMC consists of and the limited RV data, it was challenging
to produce a satisfactory velocity field. The appropriate selection of prior parameters, as well as
trying to constrain the field not to diverge into configurations that are inconsistent with the
data, proved difficult. It took many tries to arrive at somewhat promising results, and even then it
was another challenge to reproduce such results. Taurus simply has too few data points
for the algorithm to reliably constrain the field, smooth out the noisy data and deal with outlier stars
at the same time.

For this reason, this section does not present and discuss the following reconstruction of the field as the final and correct result of this thesis, but
rather as a work in progress and the most promising result acquired at this point. It is therefore also
not overly beneficial to analyze the field in detail; it is instead discussed and reviewed qualitatively.
Section~\ref{sec:Comparison} discusses different runs of the algorithm with different parameters or different quality
cuts, and even runs with the same parameters where the posterior yielded different results. Features that
appear consistently across these runs can be read as actual data driven properties rather than artefacts resulting from the choice of run.

Apart from the data cuts, the model itself was revised several times over the course of this thesis.
Most of these changes came out of a series of unsuccessful iterations, each giving further
insight into the appropriate choice of model and parameters.

First, the $\eta$ noise estimation was, after considerable deliberation, removed from the final
model. It gives the inference the freedom to downweight the already small sample of radial
velocities, whose uncertainties are far larger than those of the proper motions. Since the field
is constrained mainly by the far more precise proper motions, the noise estimation ends up
disregarding the radial velocities altogether, inflating their uncertainties to the point where
the measurements no longer constrain the field. With a sample as small as that of Taurus, this
effect is not compensated by a large number of remaining measurements, as it would be in a richer
region.

Second, the readout of the field at the stellar positions was changed in the final iterations.
Previously, each star was compared to the grid point at the same corner of the voxel
containing it, so that the point of evaluation could be displaced from the star by up to one
cell, or $3~\mathrm{pc}$, along every axis. The new readout is a trilinear interpolation: the
field is evaluated at all eight corners of the surrounding voxel and weighted by their proximity
to the star. This removes the artificial offset between a star and the field value it is compared
to, so that the response varies continuously with position and the residuals reflect genuine
disagreement between model and data rather than the discretization of the grid.

With that said, Figure~\ref{fig:vfield_gaia} presents the resulting velocity field. Vectors are drawn at
every grid cell, with a mass-density mask hiding the regions that are not covered by data
(Section~\ref{app:mass_density}). There the reconstruction is no longer constrained by the
likelihood and merely extrapolates the prior. The same mask is applied to every subsequent figure.
The general direction of the bulk motion in Figure~\ref{fig:app_uvw_vectors} remains, but with
smaller magnitudes than in the data as \texttt{NIFTy} smooths the field and filters outliers.
The main complex of Taurus appears to move in
the same direction while different parts of the cloud move at different velocities revealing subpopulations.

        represented as vectors.}

Several small-scale peaks stand out against the otherwise smooth field. They appear throughout the region,
also where stars are plentiful, since the roughly 550 members are still outnumbered by the grid cells they
have to constrain: a star whose velocity deviates from its neighbors drives a local peak rather than being
averaged down. Whether such a feature traces a real motion or a single deviant measurement is decided by its
reappearance across runs, which is examined in Section~\ref{sec:Comparison}.

The peaks also stretch the colorbar, masking the smaller
differences across the rest of the region into a narrow band of color. Figure~\ref{fig:vfield_gaia_cut}
therefore repeats the field with the colorbar clamped to $21$--$28~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, where the cells
outside this range are drawn in the limiting colors. The limits sit
roughly symmetrically around the bulk of the distribution, so that only the peaks are clipped.

    \caption{The velocity field of Figure~\ref{fig:vfield_gaia} with the colorbar clamped to
    $21$--$28~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, showing the variation between the subpopulations that the
    small-scale peaks would otherwise hide.}
    \label{fig:vfield_gaia_cut}

It should be noted that Gaia measures in the barycentric reference frame \citep{GaiaCollaboration2023}, so
the velocities describe how Taurus moves relative to the Sun. The bulk motion of the field therefore says as
much about the motion of the Sun as about that of Taurus, whereas the relative motions within the region are
unaffected by this choice of frame. To gain physically relevant information about Taurus, a change of
reference frame would be required as performed later in Section~\ref{sec:Taurus_ref}.

Comparing the velocity magnitudes to the stellar ages in Figure~\ref{fig:age_velocity} reveals a systematic
trend between the two quantities: the youngest stars (1--5~Myr) reach smaller velocity magnitudes than the
older subgroups (6--15~Myr), forming an age--velocity gradient across the region. This is not an artefact of
this particular reconstruction, as the same property appears in most other runs of the algorithm and is
already present in the stellar data itself (Figure~\ref{fig:age_vel_comp}). As in
Figure~\ref{fig:vfield_gaia_cut}, the colorbar is clipped so that the small-scale peaks do not compress the
scale of the field.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/age_velocity.png}
    \caption{Comparison between star ages (dots) and velocity magnitudes overlaid.}
    \label{fig:age_velocity}
\end{figure}

The comparison is, however, tied to the selection of reference frame. Subtracting a constant offset, as done in
Section~\ref{sec:Taurus_ref}, changes both the magnitude and the direction of every velocity vector, and the
ordering of magnitudes is not necessarily preserved under such a shift.

A 3D viewer, built for the analysis of the resulting vector field and the stellar data, is available at \href{https://dereppi.github.io/Viewer/}{vectorfield~viewer}.
Most plots shown here result from this application.

\subsection{Taurus Reference Frame}
\label{sec:Taurus_ref}
Choosing an appropriate reference frame is essential for extracting physical information about Taurus and
for any further analysis of momentum or energy. An important choice is the frame internal to the complex,
in which the motion of Taurus as a whole is removed. It is obtained by subtracting the mean field velocity,
weighted by the mass density from Section~\ref{app:mass_density}. Since the reconstruction is heliocentric,
this mean contains the motion of Taurus itself together with relative solar motion.
In this run it evaluates to $(-17.22, -14.48, -9.25)~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ in galactic coordinates,
corresponding to a bulk speed of $24.32~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Within a single run the posterior uncertainty
of this mean is small, about $0.4~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ in $U$ and below $0.1~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ in $V$ and
$W$, but between runs the values scatter by several $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ in every component. The mean motion
is therefore not a reliable measurement of the motion of Taurus, and the internal frame defined by it
inherits that limitation. For comparison, \citet{Galli2019} derive $(-16.2, -12.8, -8.9)~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$,
or $22.5~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ in magnitude, from 92 members with radial velocities. The same values enter
the reconstruction as the prior mean of the velocity offset of each component, so the agreement is not an
independent confirmation. The prior is broad enough, with a width of the order of $10~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$,
to let the data move the offset away from it, but part of the recovered mean still reflects that choice.
Figure~\ref{fig:vfield_taurus} shows the velocity field in the discussed reference frame.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/vfield_taurus.png}
    \caption{The velocity field in the Taurus internal reference frame with the bulk motion of the complex subtracted.}
    \label{fig:vfield_taurus}
\end{figure}

With the common bulk motion removed, the differences between the subgroups stand out: which parts of the
complex move faster than the region as a whole, and in which direction they depart from it.
Since an error in the subtracted mean shifts every vector by the same amount, groups can be compared
to each other within one run, while the absolute magnitudes and directions shown here carry the
uncertainty of the frame itself.

This reference frame also enables the analysis of internal energy distributions using the kinetic energy of the stars $E=\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$.
Observing where high amounts of energy are stored could reveal information about the origin of Taurus.
As the energy analysis depends on the correctness of this reference frame, a thorough investigation is not pursued here.
However, integrating $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,|\vec{v}|^2$ over the reconstruction volume, with $\rho$ the
stellar mass density of Section~\ref{app:mass_density}, gives a total kinetic energy of
$2.8\cdot10^{39}\,\mathrm{J}$ in this particular run.


\subsection{Derivatives}
\label{sec:Derivatives}

Two additional quantities derived from the field describe its local behavior: the divergence $\nabla\cdot\vec{v}$,
positive where the field expands and negative where it contracts, and the vorticity $\nabla\times\vec{v}$,
which describes local rotation and shear.

These two quantities have the advantage of being independent of the reference frame, since subtracting a
constant leaves the derivatives unchanged. They are thus not affected by the problem of the previous
section. What limits them is resolution: a derivative responds to the smallest scales
of the reconstruction, which are the least constrained by the data and also the ones that differ most
between runs (Section~\ref{sec:Comparison}). They are computed from the mean field rather than from the
individual posterior samples, so no uncertainty is attached to them.

The maps, shown in Appendix~\ref{app:derivatives}, are therefore only read qualitatively.
\citet{Galli2019} find no significant expansion or
contraction of the complex, quoting an expansion velocity of $0.0\pm0.1~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, while they do report a
potential global rotation of $1.5\pm0.1~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$.


\section{Validation}
\label{sec:Validation}
\texttt{NIFTy} provides a set of posterior samples which all represent a complete field; from these samples, a mean and uncertainty (standard deviation) field is created.
The uncertainty estimation is dependent on the current posterior.
If the algorithm is stuck in a local minimum where the posterior varies relatively little, the uncertainty is then estimated also on this local minimum, whereas the global optimum could still lie outside of this uncertainty.
It should therefore not be interpreted as deviation between runs but rather as indicator of how well the field is constrained at this point and how much it varies.
The posterior uncertainty of the run discussed above is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:uncertainty}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/vfield_std.png}
    \caption{The posterior uncertainty represented as magnitude of all three components.}
    \label{fig:uncertainty}
\end{figure}

A key insight here is that while the posterior is well constrained in the central regions, where most of the data lie, the edges have much higher uncertainties.
Another finding is that the uncertainty is dominated by the $U$~component with a median uncertainty of $0.93~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ while the $V$ and $W$ components have median uncertainties of $0.46$ and $0.57~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ respectively.
This comes from the specific property of the TMC that $U$ points almost along the line of sight and consists mostly of the radial velocity component, which has far greater measurement uncertainties than the proper motions and in turn contributes more to the variation of the posterior samples.

The residuals between the data and the field values provide another quality check on the posterior.
Stars carry peculiar motions that a continuous field cannot represent, and fitting every star
exactly is neither possible at this resolution nor desirable, so these residuals are expected to
be nonzero. Since the plain differences between stellar and field values carry only limited
information about the accuracy of the fit, the weighted residuals are more informative: each
absolute difference is divided by the effective uncertainty of the star. A weighted residual of one or
below indicates that the field matches the star within its uncertainty.
The residual statistics are presented in Table~\ref{tab:residuals}.

The most noticeable feature of this statistic is that the proper motions are clearly not fit
within their uncertainties.
The median fit lies 4.71 and 6.83 standard deviations away from the actual value, while the median
radial velocity fit lies within almost exactly one $\sigma$.
The fit is poor by this measure, but the reason is straightforward.
The effective uncertainty of the stars in this model consists purely of the measurement errors.
For the proper motions, the median error is $0.06~\mathrm{mas\,yr^{-1}}$ for $\mu_{\alpha*}$ and
$0.04~\mathrm{mas\,yr^{-1}}$ for $\mu_{\delta}$, with median measurements for $\mu_{\alpha*}$ and
$\mu_{\delta}$ of about $7$ and $-21~\mathrm{mas\,yr^{-1}}$ respectively.
So the algorithm tries to accurately fit data with an uncertainty of about one percent of the
measurement or less.
The stars, however, do not move as a continuous field; they possess peculiar motions of their own.
\citet{Galli2019} determined the three-dimensional velocity dispersion inside Taurus to be
$\sigma = 2.7~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and describe the internal motions in the radial direction of the
complex as ``dominated by random motions rather than an organized expansion or contraction
pattern''.
This suggests a change to the model that accounts for these peculiar velocities.


\subsection{Comparison}
\label{sec:Comparison}
This section serves as a case study of different iterations of the algorithm, comparing runs that differ in
their parameters, in the applied quality cuts, or merely in their results. Some of these runs show which
features of the reconstruction survive such a change and can be read as data driven, while others expose
the weaknesses of the model that motivate the extension discussed in Section~\ref{sec:peculiar_motions}.

These runs are also available at \href{https://dereppi.github.io/Viewer/}{vectorfield~viewer} with the respective
names of the runs mentioned here. To keep this work clear from yet another set of large three-dimensional plots,
the reader is advised to confirm the described features in the interactive viewer, where each run can be inspected
freely and its exact parameters are listed alongside the reconstruction.

The runs were not all produced with the same code. Over the course of this thesis several errors in the
pipeline were fixed, and the readout of the field at the stellar positions was replaced by the trilinear
interpolation described at the beginning of this chapter. A difference between two runs can therefore
originate from the parameters, from the quality cuts, or from the state of the pipeline they were run with.

The earliest iteration considered here is \texttt{parameters\_old}. It combines loose priors, which leave
the field free to vary strongly and on small scales, with quality cuts that discard more data than
necessary: only 89 stars enter the fit with a radial velocity, where the later runs keep between 110 and
160. Its noise estimation then behaves as described above: it inflates the radial-velocity errors by more
than a factor of two, leaving the field to be shaped almost entirely by the proper motions. The result is
the ``split'' of the field that appears in most runs with an active noise estimation, where the vectors
diverge in opposite directions along $U$ and separate the cloud into two halves. The data show no such
pattern. As described in Section~\ref{sec:Validation}, $U$ is the component the radial velocities
constrain; once they are downweighted, the algorithm is free to choose its direction. A strong small-scale peak in the region of the younger stars of $\sim80~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ appears alongside it. Overall,
this iteration yields no physical insight into the motions of Taurus. Its value lies in what followed from
it: the noise estimation was dropped eventually, and the quality cuts were revised to keep more of the few radial
velocities that are available.

Another interesting run is \texttt{ruwe\_test}. Its more lenient quality cuts leave 112 radial velocities, and a
steeper prior on the slope of the power spectrum should reduce the overfitting on small scales. It is the
exception among the runs with an active noise estimation, as it does not develop the patterns described
above. The median $\eta$ stays near 2, so the radial-velocity errors are inflated by some 40 per cent
instead of a factor of two or more, and the measurements keep constraining the field. Accordingly, the split along
$U$ is absent. Why the noise estimation settles at this level here and not in the other runs cannot be read
off the run itself. The field is dominated by large-scale structure and carries few small-scale
fluctuations, in line with the steeper slope prior. The age--velocity gradient described above nevertheless
reappears, which supports reading it as a property of the data rather than of one particular reconstruction.

A last example of these iterations, \texttt{test\_new\_param}, is the configuration the rewritten pipeline
started from. The noise estimation is switched off, the quality cuts are the
loosest of all runs discussed here and leave 156 radial velocities, and the prior on the fluctuations is
tightened so that the field can no longer vary as freely as before. With the radial velocities at full weight, the split along $U$ does
not appear. The mean motion, however, comes out at $U \approx -8~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, roughly half the
value of \citet{Galli2019} and of the runs discussed in Section~\ref{sec:Taurus_ref}, even though the
offset prior is centered on the literature value. A small-scale peak appears here as well, but at a
different position than in \texttt{parameters\_old}: such features move with the run rather than marking a
fixed location in the region. They persist because the field is still the only place where the deviation
of an individual star can go, which is what the next section targets.

\subsection{Peculiar motions}
\label{sec:peculiar_motions}

The demonstrations in Section~\ref{sec:Comparison} and the residuals above point at the same
weakness: the model has no way of absorbing the motion a star has on top of the large-scale
field. Every deviation has to be paid for by the field itself, so that even a small peculiar velocity turns
into a residual of several $\sigma$.

A simple approach for this is an intrinsic scatter term \citep[Sect.~8]{Hogg2010}. It starts from
the observation that the offset of a star from a smooth field has two independent causes: the
measurement error reported by Gaia, and the peculiar velocity the star has on top of the
large-scale flow. The first is known for every star, the second is not. Fitting a peculiar velocity
for each star separately would add hundreds of free parameters that the data cannot constrain, much
like the $\eta$ noise estimation discussed above. What can be captured by a single number, however,
is how widely the stars scatter as a population: their peculiar velocities are assumed to be drawn
from one common Gaussian distribution of width $\sigma_\mathrm{int}$, taken to be the same in all
directions.

Since the two contributions are independent of one another, their variances add, and since both are
Gaussian, so is their sum. A star is therefore expected to lie further from the field than its
measurement error alone would suggest, by an amount set by $\sigma_\mathrm{int}$, and the two enter
the likelihood added in quadrature. Apart from this new parameter, nothing else at the model changes,
each star still contributes a single residual between its measurement and the field, only now this
residual is judged against the widened uncertainty. For the radial velocities the effective uncertainty
is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{v_r,\mathrm{eff}}^2 = \sigma_{v_r}^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{int}^2 \, ,
    \label{eq:sigma_eff_rv}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_\mathrm{int}$ in $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. For the proper motions $\sigma_\mathrm{int}$ first
has to be converted to $\mathrm{mas\,yr^{-1}}$ at the distance $d$ of the respective star,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\mu,\mathrm{eff}}^2 = \sigma_{\mu}^2
    + \left( \frac{\sigma_\mathrm{int}}{\kappa\,d} \right)^{2} \, ,
    \qquad \kappa = 4.7405 \, ,
    \label{eq:sigma_eff_pm}
\end{equation}
with $d$ in kpc. The field is then no longer required to pass through every star; it only has to
reproduce the stellar velocities to within the width of their own velocity distribution, and the
weighted residuals of Section~\ref{sec:Validation} become a meaningful measure of the fit again.

The scale of the parameter is set by the region itself, but the $2.7~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ of
\citet{Galli2019} cannot be adopted directly: the value is three-dimensional and measures the
scatter of all Taurus members around a single mean velocity. Most of it is large-scale --- the
clouds move relative to each other by $1$ to $5~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and these coherent motions
are what the reconstructed field describes on its own. $\sigma_\mathrm{int}$ only has to cover what
the field cannot follow, the deviation of a star from its immediate surroundings on scales below
the $3~\mathrm{pc}$ of a grid cell, so the complex-wide value is an upper limit. Within the
individual clouds \citet{Galli2019} report dispersions of $0.2$ to $1.6~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ per
component, which still contain the measurement errors.

The run \texttt{test\_with\_sigma} applies this to the setup of the default run, with a log-normal prior on
$\sigma_\mathrm{int}$ of mean $0.8~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, well inside the range argued for above. The
posterior settles at $0.99~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so the data ask for slightly more scatter than the prior
suggests. Table~\ref{tab:residuals_sigma} compares the weighted residuals with those of the default run.
The absolute differences barely move, so the reconstruction is largely the same, but the median weighted
residuals of the proper motions fall from $4.71$ and $6.83$ to $0.34$ and $0.45$, and the extreme cases
from several hundred $\sigma_\mathrm{eff}$ to below ten. Both medians now lie below the $0.67$ expected
for correctly scaled uncertainties, while the radial velocities stay at $0.91$: one common width cannot
serve both at once.

Visually, the run again produces a field dominated by large scales, with no outstanding small-scale
fluctuations. This is what the model is meant to do, as it infers the smooth field underlying the stellar
motions rather than the motions themselves. One pattern stands out: large velocities in the $U$ component
on the far side of Taurus, where hardly any star with a measured radial velocity is left. Since $U$ is the
component the radial velocities constrain, the field is extrapolated there rather than measured, which
makes this an edge effect rather than a property of the region. Continuing the inference for further
iterations may also resolve it.

This run remains a test. The appropriate value of $\sigma_\mathrm{int}$, and whether inferring it alongside
the field is the right choice at all, would need further investigation; the prior used here was settled on
in a few earlier iterations by comparing the resulting fits. It should also be kept in mind that widening
the uncertainties improves the fit statistic without necessarily bringing the field closer to the data: the absolute
differences are, if anything, slightly larger than before, and only the standard they are measured against
has changed. Since
$\sigma_\mathrm{int}$ and the amplitude of the field are constrained by the same data, the inference can
trade one against the other, so the recovered $0.99~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ should not be read as a
measurement of the peculiar velocity dispersion in Taurus.

The most severe limitation, however, remains the data itself: Taurus holds few stars and even fewer with a
reliable radial velocity. Compared with pre-Gaia times the sample is immensely better in
both size and precision, but a reconstruction of this kind would profit from a revision once
Gaia~DR4 \citep{GaiaDR4}
provides a larger and better characterised set of radial velocities.
