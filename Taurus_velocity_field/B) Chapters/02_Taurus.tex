\chapter{Taurus Molecular Cloud}

\section{Structure and Properties}
\label{sec:Taurus_structure}
\citet{Zucker2022} traced
the motions of nearby young stars back in time to reconstruct the origin of the Local Bubble:
about 15~Myr ago,
massive stars of the Scorpius-Centaurus (Sco-Cen) OB association were born near what is now
the center of the Local Bubble, and their supernovae blew out the cavity. Sco-Cen has since drifted
away from the center, while the shell has kept expanding and sweeping up the surrounding
interstellar medium~(ISM) into dense structures that can subsequently collapse and form new stars.
Several nearby star-forming regions are attributed to this process, Taurus among them.

Taurus is embedded in a network of such expanding structures. It lies at the boundary between
the Local Bubble and the Perseus-Taurus (Per-Tau) shell, a roughly spherical shell of
swept-up gas and dust that connects Taurus to the neighboring Perseus molecular cloud
\citep{Bialy2021}. \citet{Soler2023} argue that the converging flows driven
by these two structures triggered the formation of the cloud. A further large cavity lies
nearby: the Orion-Eridanus superbubble, powered by the Orion~OB1 association,
extends into the broader vicinity of Taurus \citep{Ochsendorf2015}, placing the region within
reach of several large-scale structures and making it a promising source of information about
supernova-driven star formation in the solar neighborhood.

An interactive model presented by \citet{Oneill2024} depicts the Local Bubble not as a single,
fully enclosed shell, but as a system of several shells that remain open to the Galactic halo;
it is available online (\href{https://theo-oneill.github.io/localbubble/plots/bubble_enviro.html}{interactive}),
and a snapshot is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Local_Bubble}.

The TMC is one of the most intensively studied low-mass star-forming regions in the Milky Way.
It spans a total of about $15 \times 15$~degrees on the sky and lies at a distance of
approximately 140~parsecs from the Sun, although significant depth effects are present along the
line of sight: the individual clouds range from $126.6 \pm 1.7$ to $162.7 \pm 0.8$~pc. The
complex hosts a few hundred YSOs that are not randomly distributed but instead clustered into
small groups \citep{Galli2019}. Figure~\ref{fig:Taurus_sky} shows the dust emission of the
complex as seen on the sky.

The most recent census, based on Gaia~DR3, revised the total membership to 532 adopted members
distributed across 13 kinematic groups, most with median ages of $1$--$3$~Myr, while
neighboring associations have ages ranging from 13 to 56 Myr. These ages are estimated
from the offset of each group's low-mass sequence in the Hertzsprung--Russell diagram
relative to a reference population of assumed age, not from theoretical isochrones as in the
catalog described later in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}. Samples of older stars
($\geq10$ Myr) in the vicinity of Taurus were proposed to also be associated with the cloud but
were not included in the catalog because of inconsistent kinematics compared to Taurus
\citep{Luhman2023}.

\section{Kinematics}
The kinematics of Taurus have been the subject of several studies in recent years, enabled by
increasingly precise astrometric data. An early investigation by \citet{Bertout2006} used
proper motions supplemented by radial velocities to identify 94 probable members of the
Taurus-Auriga moving group and to derive individual kinematic parallaxes, yielding a mean
distance of 140~pc and a spatial velocity dispersion of approximately $6~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$
across the association.

A more complete and precise picture was established by \citet{Galli2019}, who combined Gaia~DR2
astrometry with VLBI measurements from the GOBELINS project for a sample of 415 stars. Applying
a hierarchical clustering algorithm, they identified 21 clusters, of which 13 could be
associated with distinct molecular clouds, and derived their distances and spatial velocities.
They found inter-cloud relative motions ranging from about 1 to $5~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, a
median inter-cloud separation of 25~pc, and a one-dimensional velocity dispersion among the
clouds of about $2\text{--}3~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. No significant expansion or contraction
pattern was detected for the complex as a whole, although evidence for a potential global
rotation was reported. The stellar velocities were furthermore found to closely match those of
the underlying $^{13}$CO molecular gas, confirming a tight dynamical coupling between the stars
and the gas.

This picture was extended by \citet{Soler2023}, who combined 3D dust reconstruction with CO gas
emission and neutral hydrogen observations to study the large-scale cloud dynamics. Their
analysis revealed a velocity gradient across the cloud consistent with external compression, in
line with the bubble geometry described above. Despite this progress, the internal kinematics of
Taurus remain incompletely characterized, in part because radial velocity measurements are
available for only a subset of its members. This motivates a complementary approach that uses
the full available data of Gaia proper motions, as pursued in this thesis.
